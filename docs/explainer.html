drosoXgeo · what we built, in plain words

Can a fly's brain play a jumping game?

Scientists mapped every brain cell of one real fruit fly. We turned that map into a program, and we want it to play a Geometry Dash style game using the fly's own escape reflex.

No biology background needed. Every idea below comes with a picture that moves.

1 / 6 · the plan

The fly already has a "jump!" circuit

When something rushes toward a fly, special cells in its eyes notice it getting bigger fast. They pass the alarm to one big cell, and that cell makes the fly jump away. That's how flies escape your swatter.

Geometry Dash is also a one-button jump game. So the idea is to show the fly's brain the spikes coming at it and let its own escape circuit press the button.

Like plugging a game controller into a brain, using the wire the brain already has for jumping.

2 / 6 · one brain cell

A brain cell is a leaky bucket

A brain cell (a neuron) gets tiny messages from other cells. Each message pours a little water into its bucket. The bucket has a hole, so water slowly drains out.

If the water reaches the line, the cell fires: it sends its own message to all the cells it's connected to, and its bucket empties.

Try the slider. With few messages the hole wins and the cell stays quiet. With many, it fires again and again.

That is the whole rule. Scientists call it "leaky integrate-and-fire": leaky (the hole), integrate (the filling), fire (the overflow).

The wiggly line at the bottom is the water level over time. Neuroscientists record exactly this from real cells and call it voltage.

3 / 6 · the map

The wiring is what makes it work

Wire many buckets together and you have a brain. The fly's map says exactly who is wired to whom: 139,000 cells and 15 million connections.

Today we ran a real experiment from a 2024 paper. We poked the cells that taste sugar and watched the cell that starts eating. Taste sugar, start eating. It fired.

Then the important part. We scrambled the wiring: every cell kept the same number of connections, but to random partners. The sugar cells still fired, yet the eating cell went completely silent.

Same number of roads in the city, but shuffled at random. The cars still drive, but nobody reaches the restaurant.

5 / 6 · how fast is your Mac?

Fast enough, if the brain stays calm

We timed how long your laptop takes to simulate one second of fly life.

When only a few senses are active, it's about twice as fast as real life. When a lot of the brain is buzzing, it's a little slower than real life.

This matters only at the very end, when the brain plays the real game, because the real game won't wait. Our copy of the game waits for the brain, so for now speed doesn't block anything.

6 / 6 · what's next

Next: the same test, for jumping

Today we showed: poke the sugar cells, the eating cell fires. Next we try: poke the "something's coming" cells, does the jump cell fire?

As the spike gets closer it looks bigger to the fly. The "something's coming" cells fire faster and faster. If the jump cell fires at the right moment, we press jump.

And we'll run the same scrambled-wiring check, so we know it's the fly's wiring doing it.
